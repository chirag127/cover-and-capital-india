## 1. Why CAGR Fails for Insurance Policies
The Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) formula is defined strictly for a single lump-sum cash flow:

$$\text{CAGR} = \left( \frac{\text{Ending Value}}{\text{Beginning Value}} \right)^{\frac{1}{n}} - 1$$

When insurance agents evaluate a 10-year limited pay endowment policy where you invest ₹1,00,000 every year for 10 years and receive ₹18,00,000 at year 16, they often state:
$$\text{Total Invested} = \text{INR } 10,00,000, \quad \text{Maturity} = \text{INR } 18,00,000, \quad \text{Years} = 16$$
$$\text{Falsely Computed CAGR} = \left( \frac{18,00,000}{10,00,000} \right)^{\frac{1}{16}} - 1 \approx \mathbf{3.75\%}$$
Or worse, they divide the ₹8 Lakh gain by 10 years and claim an "8% annual return".

Both numbers are mathematically invalid because the ₹1,00,000 paid in Year 9 was invested for only 7 years, while the ₹1,00,000 paid in Year 1 was invested for 15 years.

To account for the **time value of money across staggered cash flows**, you must calculate the **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**.

---

## 2. Mathematical Definition of IRR
The Internal Rate of Return ($r$) is the specific discount rate that equates the **Net Present Value (NPV)** of all cash inflows and outflows to zero:

$$\text{NPV}(r) = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1 + r)^t} = 0$$

Where:
* $\text{CF}_t$ is the net cash flow at time $t$. (Negative for premiums paid; positive for survival payouts, dividends, or maturity sums).
* $t$ is the time interval in years ($t = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n$).
* $r$ is the internal rate of return per period.

Because this equation is a polynomial of degree $n$, it cannot generally be solved algebraically when $n \ge 5$ (Abel-Ruffini theorem). It must be solved numerically via iterative root-finding methods.

---

## 3. The Newton-Raphson Solver Algorithm
The standard numerical method used in spreadsheet software (`=IRR()`) and Cover & Capital's TypeScript engine is the **Newton-Raphson method**:

$$r_{k+1} = r_k - \frac{f(r_k)}{f'(r_k)}$$

Where:
$$f(r) = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_t}{(1 + r)^t}$$
And the first derivative with respect to $r$ is:
$$f'(r) = -\sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{t \cdot \text{CF}_t}{(1 + r)^{t+1}}$$

### Step-by-Step Numerical Walkthrough:
Consider: Pay ₹1,00,000 at $t=0$, ₹1,00,000 at $t=1$, receive ₹2,35,000 at $t=2$.
1. Initial guess: $r_0 = 0.10$ (10%).
2. Compute $f(0.10) = -100000 - \frac{100000}{1.10} + \frac{235000}{1.10^2} = -100000 - 90909.09 + 194214.88 = +3305.79$.
3. Compute derivative $f'(0.10) = -\frac{100000}{1.10^2} - \frac{2 \times 235000}{1.10^3} = -82644.63 - 353117.96 = -435762.59$.
4. Next approximation:
$$r_1 = 0.10 - \frac{3305.79}{-435762.59} = 0.10 + 0.007586 = \mathbf{0.107586 \ (10.76\%)}$$
5. Repeating for iteration 2 converges rapidly to the exact solution: $r \approx 10.78\%$.

## 4. XIRR for Irregular Calendar Dates
Standard IRR assumes perfectly equal annual or monthly intervals. However, real insurance policies have irregular cash-flow dates due to leap years, non-annual premium modes, or mid-term survival benefits.

The **Extended Internal Rate of Return (XIRR)** uses actual calendar dates under the **ACT/365 convention**:

$$\sum_{i=0}^{n} \frac{\text{CF}_i}{(1 + r)^{\frac{d_i - d_0}{365.0}}} = 0$$

Where $d_i$ is the actual calendar date of transaction $i$, and $d_0$ is the date of the initial premium payment.

## 5. Practical Checklist for Policy Audits
When evaluating any policy illustration from LIC, HDFC Life, ICICI Pru, or Max Life:
1. Export the table of year-by-year cash flows into a spreadsheet.
2. Verify that all premium entries have a **negative sign** (`-₹1,00,000`).
3. Verify that all survival payouts and maturity values have a **positive sign** (`+₹1,50,000`).
4. Apply `=XIRR(values, dates)` using exact dates.
5. Compare the resulting XIRR against the risk-free **10-Year Government of India Bond (7.1%)** and **Public Provident Fund (7.1% tax-free)**.
